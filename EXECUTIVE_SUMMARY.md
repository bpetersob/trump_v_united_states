### Executive Summary

The Supreme Court's 2024 ruling in *Trump v. United States* would be viewed by the Founding Fathers as a grave and dangerous error, even if it stopped short of granting total immunity. Their critiques would focus on the specifics of the new legal framework and its violation of the core principles of accountability they established.

*   **George Washington** would see the grant of "absolute immunity" for core powers [[3]](https://www.oyez.org/cases/2023/23-939)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_v._United_States)[[7]](https://pennlawreview.com/2026/03/06/power-and-immunity-in-youngstown-and-trump-v-united-states/)  as a step toward the monarchy he disdained [[19]](https://www.loc.gov/exhibitions/two-georges/about-this-exhibition/president-and-king/)[[11]](https://www.quora.com/Did-George-Washington-see-himself-as-President-or-King-and-which-did-he-prefer-for-the-future-of-America) . He would view it as a betrayal of his life's work to establish a president who is a servant of the people and bound by the Constitution, which he held as "sacredly obligatory upon all" [[17]](https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/george-washington-on-respecting-the-constitution-as-written/)[[18]](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/washing.asp) .
*   **Thomas Jefferson** would view the ruling as the predictable work of the "despotic branch" he always feared—the judiciary [[32]](https://www.facebook.com/groups/339317480411211/posts/1838450463831231/) . He would point to his aggressive prosecution of Aaron Burr as proof of his belief that no official is above the law, and argue that the Court has ignored the fundamental republican principle that a president acting beyond the law must still "throw himself on the justice of his country" [[39]](https://www.americanyawp.com/text/07-the-early-republic/)[[41]](https://drive.google.com/a/google.com/open?id=1QoQPtsEWL1-o3Yi430myawIFC3muPEXGKg6ElbMCs8U)[[38]](https://www.britannica.com/today-in-history/May-22-Aaron-Burrs-Treason-Trial-Begins)[[37]](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-great-trial-that-tested-the-constitutions-treason-clause) .
*   **John Adams** would decry the multi-tiered framework [[7]](https://pennlawreview.com/2026/03/06/power-and-immunity-in-youngstown-and-trump-v-united-states/)  as the death of his most cherished principle: "a government of laws, and not of men" [[65]](https://www.mass.gov/guides/john-adams-the-massachusetts-constitution)[[66]](https://allthingsliberty.com/2022/05/john-adams-and-the-rule-of-law/) . He would argue it replaces the universal supremacy of law with arbitrary judicial judgment, noting that even his own controversial enforcement of the Alien and Sedition Acts was authorized by Congress, not protected by a pre-existing legal doctrine of immunity [[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_and_Sedition_Acts)[[43]](https://drive.google.com/a/google.com/open?id=1P4ZpJ5pi4t0L9xO162vrx6Qw2tLbj1lSJ7-b1IISsVs)[[91]](https://millercenter.org/president/john-adams/key-events) .
*   **James Madison** would see the ruling as a structural collapse of his system where "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" [[103]](https://billofrightsinstitute.org/primary-sources/federalist-no-51/)[[107]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalist_No._51) . By removing the judiciary's "constitutional means" to check the executive, the Court has dangerously weakened the bulwarks against the "encroaching spirit of power," a danger he explicitly warned of at the Constitutional Convention [[105]](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed48.asp)[[106]](https://ballotpedia.org/Federalist_No._48_by_James_Madison_(1788))[[114]](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_720.asp)[[113]](https://www.cato.org/white-paper/indispensable-remedy-broad-scope-constitutions-impeachment-power) .
*   **Alexander Hamilton**, the advocate for a strong executive, would be horrified to see his call for "energy" twisted into a justification for impunity [[120]](https://billofrightsinstitute.org/primary-sources/federalist-no-70/) . He would point directly to his writings in Federalist No. 69, where he explicitly stated that a president, unlike a king, would be liable to "prosecution and punishment in the ordinary course of law" after removal from office [[125]](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed69.asp)[[126]](https://talkingpointsmemo.com/edblog/yes-hamilton-thought-john-roberts-was-full-of-it-too)[[127]](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/federalist-no-69-the-real-character-the-executive) .

In unison, they would likely agree with the dissenting justices [[3]](https://www.oyez.org/cases/2023/23-939)[[4]](https://ballotpedia.org/Trump_v._United_States)[[144]](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2024/07/01/sotomayor-jackson-trump-immunity-dissent/)[[145]](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2024/07/sotomayor-jackson-blistering-dissents-trump-immunity-case/) : by creating special legal protections for the president, the Court has damaged the fundamental principle that no person in the republic is above the law, risking the very foundation of the nation they built [[6]](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/read-the-full-supreme-court-decision-on-trump-and-presidential-immunity)[[3]](https://www.oyez.org/cases/2023/23-939) .
